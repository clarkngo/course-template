## The Offer Is a Starting Position, Not a Verdict

Most first-time negotiators treat a job offer the way they'd treat a grade on a test — a fixed evaluation of their worth that either feels fair or feels like an insult, full stop. That framing makes negotiating feel like arguing with a verdict, which is uncomfortable enough that a lot of people just don't do it.

The more accurate framing: an offer is an opening position in a negotiation both sides expect to happen. Recruiters build room into initial offers precisely because they anticipate a counter — a number that goes unnegotiated often means money was left on the table, not that the number was perfectly calibrated to your exact value. This lesson is about negotiating that room back, respectfully and without damaging the relationship you're about to start.

---

## Anchoring: Why the First Number Matters So Much

**Anchoring** is the finding that the first number stated in a negotiation exerts outsized influence on where it ends up, even on people who know about the effect and are actively trying to resist it. Whoever states a number first sets the gravitational center that the rest of the conversation orbits around.

This has one direct practical implication: **avoid being the one to name a number first**, especially early in the process, when a recruiter asks for your salary expectations before making an offer. If you say "$130k" and they were prepared to offer $150k, you've just anchored the entire negotiation ten to twenty thousand dollars below where it could have started — and you'll likely never find out.

A few ways to deflect the "what are you expecting" question without being evasive or difficult:

- **Redirect to their range.** "I'd love to hear the range you're working with for this role first, so we can make sure we're in the same ballpark before I share a number."
- **If pushed, give a wide, researched range, not a single figure.** A single number reads as a ceiling you'll negotiate down from; a range reads as informed and leaves room.
- **Cite research, not a guess.** "Based on what I'm seeing for this role and level in this market, I'd expect somewhere in the $X to $Y range" is harder to dismiss than an unsourced number, and signals you've done homework.

Once *they've* made an offer, the anchor has already been set by them — and now you get to counter it, which is a much stronger position than naming the first number yourself would have been.

## What's Actually Negotiable Beyond Base Salary

Base salary is the most obvious lever and often the least flexible one — many companies have banding that a recruiter genuinely cannot move far past without a separate approval process. Total compensation has several other levers, some of which move more easily than base:

| Lever | How flexible it usually is |
|---|---|
| Base salary | Often tightly banded; harder to move |
| Signing bonus | Frequently the easiest lever — one-time, no long-term budget impact |
| Equity / stock grant | Flexible at some companies, rigid at others by level |
| Start date | Almost always negotiable, costs the company nothing |
| Title / level | Hard to move post-offer, but worth raising if you have a specific case |
| Remote / hybrid arrangement | Depends heavily on team policy, worth asking explicitly |
| PTO | Sometimes negotiable, especially if you're forfeiting accrued PTO elsewhere |

The practical takeaway: if you hear "we can't move on base," that's not the end of the conversation, it's information about *which* lever to try next. "Understood on base — is there flexibility on the signing bonus, or on start date given I have some vested equity vesting at my current company in six weeks?" keeps the conversation open on levers the recruiter may actually have discretion over.

## Countering Respectfully: A Real Script

The mechanics of a counter matter less than most people fear — recruiters handle this conversation constantly and it rarely damages goodwill when done professionally. A structure that works:

1. **Express genuine enthusiasm first.** This isn't just politeness — it removes any ambiguity about whether you're negotiating because you're unsure about the role, versus negotiating because you want the numbers to reflect your value.
2. **State your ask as a specific number, with a reason.** Vague asks ("can you do better?") invite vague responses; specific asks with grounding invite real answers.
3. **Leave room to be flexible on the structure**, even if not the total. Naming what you actually care about most helps the recruiter find a path to yes.

**Script:**
> "Thank you so much for the offer — I'm genuinely excited about this role and the team. I did want to see if there's room to move on base. Based on my research for this level in this market, and given [a specific relevant factor — a competing offer, a specific skill, years of directly relevant experience], I was hoping for something closer to $X. I'm flexible on how we get there — whether that's base, signing bonus, or equity — happy to talk through what's possible."

Notice what this script does *not* do: it doesn't threaten to walk, doesn't invent a fake competing offer, and doesn't attack the initial number as insulting. It states a specific ask, grounds it in something real, and explicitly signals flexibility on structure — which gives the recruiter multiple paths to say yes instead of one rigid one to say no to.

<Pitfall title="Treating Negotiation as Adversarial">

**Weaker:** "That number is lower than I expected, honestly. I have other offers I'm considering and I need you to come up significantly or I'll probably go elsewhere."

Even when partly true, this framing puts the recruiter in a defensive position and invites them to test the bluff rather than collaborate on a number — and if there isn't actually another offer, it's a real risk if asked for specifics.

**Better:** "I'm really excited about this offer and where I'd be able to contribute here. I am weighing another opportunity that's come in higher on base, and I'd genuinely prefer to join this team if we can get closer — is there room to move?" This version states the same underlying fact (a competing offer exists) without the ultimatum framing, keeps the relationship collaborative, and is something you can actually stand behind if asked a follow-up question. Negotiation that damages the relationship before you've even started the job is a bad trade even when it works.

</Pitfall>

## Try This

If you have a real offer in hand, or a target role in mind, draft your counter using the script structure above:

1. Write your specific ask as a number, with the concrete reason behind it — market research, a competing offer, a specific skill or experience level.
2. Identify one lever beyond base salary you'd accept as part of the total package, in case base genuinely can't move.
3. Say the whole script out loud once before any real conversation — awkward phrasing is much easier to catch and fix in a rehearsal than live on a call.
