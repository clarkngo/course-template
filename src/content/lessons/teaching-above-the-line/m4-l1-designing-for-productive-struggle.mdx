## The Thing AI Removes

There's a specific mental state where learning happens: the effortful gap between not understanding something and understanding it — wrestling with a problem, holding confusion, trying an approach that fails, adjusting. Cognitive scientists call the deliberate version of this **desirable difficulty** or **productive struggle**. It's uncomfortable, it's slow, and it is exactly where a durable skill gets built.

A general-purpose chatbot is, by default, a *struggle-removal machine.* Ask it anything and it resolves your uncertainty instantly. That feels like help, and for an expert it often is. For a learner it can be the opposite: the relief of the answer arrives *before* the struggle that would have built the skill. The student feels the pleasant sensation of a resolved problem without having done the work that resolution was supposed to represent.

## Why the Brain Fights You

Kallas gives the sharpest reason this matters, and it's biological. The brain is the body's single largest energy consumer, and higher-order thinking is *metabolically expensive* — so the brain **prefers the lower cognitive mode**, the one it can run on autopilot, because it costs less energy. In her words, studying for a hard exam leaves you "dead next day physically," because the brain ran for hours in a high cognitive mode; it's more draining than running a marathon.

This reframes your whole job as an activity designer. Productive struggle isn't uncomfortable by accident — it's uncomfortable because it's *energetically costly*, and the brain is wired to avoid the cost. A frictionless AI is so seductive precisely because it lets the brain stay in the cheap, low-energy mode while still producing finished work. You are not designing against laziness; you are designing against *thermodynamics*. That's why keeping students in higher-order thinking takes deliberate structure — left alone, per Kallas, learners "leap back to the usual lower cognitive thinking," and so does any system running on inertia.

Which is exactly why she reaches the counterintuitive conclusion that AI belongs *in* the classroom: used well, the tool is the thing that *pushes* students up into the expensive mode they'd otherwise avoid. So an activity designer's core question becomes:

> **Does this activity keep the student in the productive struggle, or does it offer a frictionless exit from it?**

## Why Estonia Built a Tutor, Not a Chatbot

The instructive case is Estonia's **AI Leap** (*AI-hüpe*), the national program that put AI into upper-secondary schools starting in February 2025 (extending to vocational students in September 2026). The telling decision: rather than hand students off-the-shelf ChatGPT, Estonia co-developed a **Socratic version of ChatGPT with OpenAI** — deliberately *not* the general-purpose tool, but one tuned to tutor rather than answer. As Kallas puts it, it's "a Socratic version of ChatGPT for schools," and the country is running it as a live experiment with scientific monitoring, because "there is very little science that we know about it" yet.

The difference between a stock chatbot and a Socratic tutor is entirely in the design goals. A stock assistant optimizes for *user satisfaction*: give the answer, quickly, completely. A learning tutor optimizes for *learning*, which sometimes means deliberately **withholding** the answer. The design criteria that fall out of that inversion:

- **Notice mistakes, but don't just correct them** — surface that something's off and let the student find it.
- **Ask the student to explain their reasoning** before offering help — reasoning-first, not answer-first.
- **Normalize error** — treat being wrong as part of the process, so students stay in the struggle instead of bailing to the answer.
- **Don't overwhelm** — small, digestible nudges rather than a wall of complete solution.
- **Withhold the finished answer** when struggling is the point.

You don't need to build a custom tutor to use these. They're **prompt-design and activity-design principles** you can apply with any tool: when you set up an AI-assisted activity, you're specifying *how* the tool should behave, and "Socratic tutor that asks before it tells" is a stance you can instruct into almost any model.

<Pitfall title="Frictionless Is a Design Choice, Not a Fact About AI">

It's easy to talk about "AI" as if instant-answer behavior were an immutable property of the technology. It isn't — it's a *default*, chosen to make consumer products feel helpful. The same model, differently instructed, will ask you questions, refuse to hand over the answer, and coach you through your own reasoning. When you design an activity, you are choosing which behavior the student meets. Defaulting to the consumer chatbot is a design decision to remove struggle; you can decide otherwise.

</Pitfall>

## Turning the Principles Into Activities

Concretely, these principles reshape the activities you design:

- **Reasoning-first prompts.** The activity requires the student to state their thinking *before* the tool responds — mirroring "attempt before assistance" from the loop, now baked into the moment-to-moment interaction.
- **AI-as-coach, not AI-as-oracle.** You instruct the tool (and the student) that its job is to ask the next good question, not to deliver the conclusion. The student stays the author.
- **Calibrated hints.** Design help to arrive in graduated steps — a nudge, then a bigger nudge — so students get *unstuck* without getting *rescued*. The struggle is preserved; only the dead-ends are removed.
- **Error as material.** Build activities where a wrong answer is the interesting part — where students examine, explain, and learn from mistakes (their own or the model's) rather than racing past them.

The unifying idea: you are not designing activities that *use* AI or *avoid* AI. You are designing activities that keep the student in the effortful state where learning happens, and instructing the tool to protect that state instead of dissolving it.

<div>

**The setup optimizes for finishing, not learning — it hands students a frictionless exit from the struggle. The minimal fix is to change the tool's instructed behavior: make it a reasoning-first coach that asks before it tells.**

'Ask it whatever you need to finish' invites the student to trade the productive struggle for an instant, complete answer — the struggle-removal default. You don't need a custom tutor or a ban; you need to re-instruct the same tool. Give it a Socratic system prompt: require students to explain their reasoning first, have the model surface (but not fix) mistakes, and deliver graduated hints instead of finished solutions. Same tool, inverted goal — from satisfying the user to keeping them in the flow of thinking.
