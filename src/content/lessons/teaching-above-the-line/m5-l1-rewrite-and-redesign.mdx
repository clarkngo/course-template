## Start Where the Course Actually Starts

You've collected lenses — the moved line, the re-priced ladder, load-bearing foundations, grade-the-thinking assessment, the five-step loop, productive struggle, Socratic tools, metacognition. This capstone assembles them. But assembly has an order, and it starts at the one place everything else derives from: the **learning objectives.**

Objectives are the load-bearing wall. Activities and assessments are supposed to *align* to them — this is Wiggins and McTighe's backward design, and it's not optional here. If your objective still lives below the line, no amount of clever assessment redesign fixes the course; you're building above-the-line scaffolding on a below-the-line foundation. So the redesign begins by rewriting the verbs.

---

## Rewriting the Verbs

Bloom's taxonomy is, usefully, a **list of verbs.** The level of an objective is encoded in its verb, so rewriting the level is often as concrete as swapping the verb — and then rebuilding the task to honor the new one.

Walk your objectives against the ladder and move the verb up:

| Below the line (AI does it) | Above the line (student must think) |
|---|---|
| *Define* the key terms | *Distinguish* which term applies to an ambiguous new case and defend it |
| *Summarize* the reading | *Critique* the reading's strongest claim and where it fails |
| *Explain* how the process works | *Evaluate* two processes and justify a choice under real constraints |
| *Apply* the formula to the problem | *Design* a solution for a novel local case and defend the trade-offs |
| *List* the causes | *Argue* which cause is primary and rebut the strongest objection |

Two guardrails as you rewrite:

- **The foundation didn't leave the course; it left the *objective's verb.*** "Distinguish which term applies" still requires knowing the terms cold — you're still teaching definitions (Module 2's *teach the foundation, assess above it*). You've moved what's *graded*, not what's *taught*.
- **Verb-swapping isn't cosmetic.** Changing "summarize" to "critique" on paper while the assignment still rewards a summary is worse than doing nothing — it hides a below-the-line task behind an above-the-line label. The verb change is a promise the assessment has to keep.

<Pitfall title="Beware the Bloom's Ladder as a Literal Staircase">

The taxonomy is a design tool, not a neuroscience claim. Humans don't switch cleanly between a "lower brain mode" and a "higher brain mode," and real thinking braids the levels together constantly — you analyze *using* recalled facts, you create *by* evaluating options. Use the ladder to audit *where your graded evidence sits and whether AI can supply it*, which is what it's genuinely good for. Don't use it to claim you've turned off students' lower-order processing or to build a rigid six-rung staircase every lesson must climb in order. It's a lens on your assessment, not a map of the brain.

</Pitfall>

## Aligning the Three Layers

With objectives above the line, align the other two layers to them — this is the whole course, integrated:

1. **Objective** (this lesson): a higher-order verb, with the foundation named as required equipment.
2. **Activities** (Module 4): productive struggle that exercises that verb — Socratic tools, calibrated hints, error-as-material — with a metacognitive reflection layer.
3. **Assessment** (Module 3): graded evidence of the thinking, built with the five-step loop so the honest path beats the shortcut.

When the three agree, the course has a property worth naming: **a student who lets AI think instead of them cannot earn the grade, and a student who thinks with AI is exactly the higher-order thinker the objective describes.** The cheat path and the learning path have converged, all the way up.

## The Capstone

<CodeLab title="Assemble Your Redesigned Unit">

Produce a one-page redesign of the unit you've carried since Module 1. It must contain:

1. **The original** — the task and its Bloom's level, plus your Module-1 AI test result (how well a model did it, what the student learned).
2. **Rewritten objective(s)** — above the line, with the higher-order verb, and a sentence naming the foundational knowledge still taught as equipment.
3. **The assessment** — your five-step-loop conversion from Module 3 (at least three steps), showing what thinking each graded artifact now makes visible.
4. **The activity + tool stance** — the productive-struggle activity and the Socratic instruction from Module 4.
5. **The metacognitive layer** — the two tool-use reflection questions you'll grade.
6. **The AI test, re-run** — paste the *new* task into a model. Show that straight-through AI now produces work that's worse, incomplete, or exposes the student who didn't think.

Item 6 is the proof. If a model still aces the redesigned unit with no thinking, you're not above the line yet — iterate.

</CodeLab>

## The Leap, and Your Part in It

Kallas frames higher-order thinking as an evolutionary challenge — a chance to "consciously train as many people as possible to be constantly in the flow of higher cognitive processes." The word doing the work is *consciously.* Higher-order thinkers don't emerge because AI arrived; if anything, the frictionless tool pushes the other way, toward outsourced judgment and lost agency. They emerge because someone deliberately designs the courses that demand and build that thinking.

That someone is the instructor and the course designer. Not the AI vendor, not the detection service, not the policy office — the person who decides what a course asks a human to do. This course has been a set of tools for that job. The line moved up the ladder; your work is to move the courses up to meet it.

<Quiz question="A colleague rewrites every objective's verb to 'create' or 'evaluate,' but leaves the assignments and grading rubrics untouched. Why has the redesign failed, and what's the tell?">
<div>

**Objectives, activities, and assessment are no longer aligned — an above-the-line label sits on below-the-line work, which is worse than the honest original because it hides the gap. The tell is the re-run AI test: a model still aces the assignments.**

Backward design requires the three layers to agree; changing only the verb is a cosmetic promise the assessment doesn't keep. If the rubric still rewards a competent summary, the objective's word 'evaluate' is decoration — the graded evidence still lives on a rung AI completes for free. The diagnostic is item 6 of the capstone: paste the *actual* assignment into a model. If it succeeds without thinking, the objective's verb is fiction and the redesign hasn't happened yet, regardless of what the syllabus says.

</div>
</Quiz>

## You're Done

You have a repeatable method: read your objectives against the re-priced ladder, rewrite the verbs above the line while keeping foundations as equipment, align activities for productive struggle and assessments that grade thinking, and verify with the AI test. Run it on the next unit, then the next. That's the leap — one course at a time.
