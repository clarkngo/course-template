## Where To Push

You've mapped the system, traced its loops, and surfaced whose problem you're solving. Now the design question: **where do you intervene?** Not every point in a system yields the same return on a push. Donella Meadows spent a career on exactly this, and left a ranked list of **leverage points** — places to intervene, ordered from least to most powerful.

The human-centered reading of her list has a clear punchline: **the shallow, easy leverage points are the ones made of numbers, and the deep, powerful ones are the ones made of people.** Teams gravitate to the shallow ones because they're legible and safe, and then wonder why nothing really changed.

---

## Meadows' Points, From Shallow to Deep

Her full list has twelve entries; the pattern matters more than memorizing them. Grouped and simplified:

**Shallowest — parameters.** Tax rates, buffer sizes, the numeric target on a dashboard, staffing levels. These are the knobs everyone reaches for first *because* they're adjustable — and they move the system least. Meadows' famous jab: parameters are "diddling with the details, arranging the deck chairs on the Titanic." Changing a quota from 40 to 45 does not change the system that made quota the goal.

**Middle — structure and information flows.** Who can see what; who is connected to whom; the length of delays; the rules and incentives. This is more powerful, and it's deeply human: **giving a person access to information they were missing, or a feedback signal they couldn't see, changes behavior far more than changing their target.** Much of humane design lives here — restoring a signal the system had hidden from the people in it.

**Deepest — goals and paradigms.** The goal of the system itself, and the shared mindset the goal comes from. This is the most powerful leverage and the least accessible from a dashboard, because it's made entirely of what people believe the system is *for*. Change a call center's goal from "minimize handle time" to "resolve the customer's actual problem" and every shallower lever — metrics, scripts, staffing — reorganizes underneath it. You changed one sentence about purpose, and the whole system followed.

---

## The Human-Centered Re-Reading

Line Meadows' hierarchy up against the parts of a sociotechnical system and a pattern jumps out:

| Leverage depth | What it's made of | Human-centered move |
|---|---|---|
| Shallow (parameters) | Numbers | Adjust a target, a rate, a headcount |
| Middle (structure/info) | Connections & signals | Give people a signal or discretion they lacked |
| Deep (goals/paradigms) | Shared beliefs about purpose | Change what the system is *for* |

The deeper you go, the more the leverage point *is* the people. This is why purely technical fixes so often disappoint: they operate on the shallow, numeric layer, while the leverage lives in the layer made of human goals and beliefs — the layer a code change can't reach.

<Pitfall title="Reaching for the Parameter Because It's the Only Knob You Control">

The gravitational pull toward shallow leverage isn't stupidity — it's that parameters are the part you're *allowed* to change without a hard conversation. You can adjust a threshold this afternoon; you cannot change your org's definition of success without touching politics, ego, and someone's OKRs. So teams optimize the knob they can reach and avoid the lever that would actually work. Naming this honestly — "we're tuning a parameter because the real leverage is a goal none of us can change today" — is more useful than pretending the parameter is the fix. At least then everyone knows the deck chairs are being rearranged.

</Pitfall>

---

## Choosing a Humane Intervention

When you pick where to push, run each candidate through two filters:

1. **How deep is this leverage point?** Parameter, structure/information, or goal? Deeper is more powerful — and usually harder and slower. Sometimes a parameter is genuinely the right call for now; just know that's what you chose and don't expect it to change the system.

2. **What does it do to the people inside the system — their signal, their discretion, their purpose?** The most humane and most powerful interventions tend to be the same ones: *give a person back a signal or a decision the system had taken from them.* That's a middle-to-deep leverage point that also treats the human as a capable part of the system rather than a component to be constrained. Watch out for the inverse — interventions that gain leverage by removing human discretion. They sometimes work, but they're where sociotechnical systems most often turn against the people in them.

<CodeLab title="Locate Your Leverage">

List the interventions you're currently considering for your running example. For each, mark its depth: **parameter**, **structure/information**, or **goal/paradigm**.

You'll probably find most of them are parameters — that's the normal gravitational pull. Now force one question: **what is the deepest leverage point available here, and what's stopping you from using it?** Often the honest answer is "the real leverage is a goal I don't control." Write that down explicitly. Then, for whatever you *can* do, prefer the intervention that gives a person back a signal or a decision the system had hidden — the humane middle lever — over one that just tunes a number or removes someone's discretion.

</CodeLab>

Options: (a) lower the delivery-time target, (b) show drivers their own real-time fatigue and route-risk data and let them adjust, (c) change the company goal from 'packages per hour' to 'safe on-time delivery.' Ranked by Meadows-style leverage, which is deepest, and why does it matter here?">
<div>

**(c) is deepest — it changes the system's goal — and it matters because as long as the goal is 'packages per hour,' options (a) and (b) get overridden by the pressure that goal creates.**

Lowering the time target (a) is a parameter tweak — the shallowest lever; drivers still race because the underlying goal still rewards racing. Giving drivers their own fatigue and risk data (b) is a genuine middle lever: it restores a signal and hands discretion back to the human, which is why it's both more powerful and more humane than (a). But (c) is deepest, because the goal is what generates the pressure that makes drivers ignore both a lower target and their own fatigue data. Change 'packages per hour' to 'safe on-time delivery' and the shallower levers finally start working with you instead of against you — one sentence about purpose reorganizes everything beneath it. In practice you might do (b) now (you can) while pushing for (c) (the real fix), naming honestly that the parameter and even the signal are second-best to changing what the system is for.
